# The Story of the Drowning World

The spine every line of dialogue is written against. Nothing here is exposition the player is
told directly — it is what the writers know so the fragments cohere.

## The premise

This is Earth, long after us. The sea has been rising for six generations. Nobody alive
remembers a coastline that stayed put. The seven wonders still stand because they were built on
high ground or built impossibly well; everything between them is gone.

The people left are not survivors of a disaster. They are the descendants of people who stopped
running. The water rises about a hand's width a year — slow enough to live with, fast enough
that every family has a house their grandparents built that is now underwater.

## Tone

Elegiac, warm, domestic. Not apocalyptic. Nobody is screaming; they are mending nets, arguing
about salt, teaching a child to swim in a flooded kitchen. The horror is entirely in the
ordinariness. Humour is allowed and encouraged — these are people, not symbols.

**Never**: heroic quests, villains, chosen ones, science lectures, climate sermons.
**Always**: specific objects, specific weather, specific dead relatives, specific fish.

## The five stages of revelation

Dialogue is gated by how much the player has discovered. Each stage is a deeper layer, not a
different subject.

- **Stage 0 — Weather and work.** Nothing about the flood. Fish, rope, a leaking roof, whether
  the wind will turn. Establishes that life continues.
- **Stage 1 — The water is a character.** Casual references to it rising. "That was a street."
  "We moved up in my mother's time." Said the way we'd mention rent.
- **Stage 2 — What was lost.** Specific, personal, small. A boat named after a sister. A door
  they still swim down to touch. The submerged settlements become legible.
- **Stage 3 — The wonders.** Who built them, what they were for, the fact that nobody agrees.
  Half-remembered, mangled, argued over. A child's version and an elder's version differ, and
  the elder is not necessarily right.
- **Stage 4 — What people do with an ending.** Not a solution. Whether to climb higher, whether
  to teach children to read, whether it matters. This is where the game earns its ending.

## Voices

**Children (Kalyani, Ravi, Meera, Tan)** — Short sentences. Present tense. No nostalgia; they
have never seen the water lower, so the flood is not a tragedy, it is just the world. They ask
the player questions. They are the only ones who find the ruins *fun*. Wrong facts stated with
total confidence.

**Teens (Bhaskar, Ilo)** — Restless. The only ones who talk about leaving. Embarrassed by their
elders' rituals but perform them anyway. Sharpest observers; they notice what adults have
stopped seeing.

**Adults (Anzu, Devi, Hana, Marek, Odd, Pell, Suri)** — Talk in tasks. Deflect feeling into
logistics: tide tables, salvage weight, whose turn it is to bail. Grief arrives sideways, in a
sentence about rope. They are the ones holding it together and they are tired.

**Elders (Amma Vasi, Old Neru)** — The only ones with a before. Circle back. Contradict
themselves and don't care. Casually reveal enormous things while discussing something trivial.
Amma Vasi is warm and gossipy; Old Neru is dry and a bit cruel and secretly the most hopeful.

## Places, and what they mean

- **Waterline settlements** — stilts, rafts, pontoons. The stubborn. They bail every day. They
  regard hill people as cowards and say so fondly.
- **Shore** — half-abandoned. People who moved once and will have to move again. The saddest
  places, because they are mid-decision.
- **Upland / mountain** — the ones who left early and feel both safe and guilty. Colder, thinner
  living. They keep records.
- **Caves** — the oldest continuous habitation. Where things get stored: seeds, books, the dead.
- **Drowned ruins** — no residents. Their story is told by the people nearby who used to live in
  them, and by the environmental lore when the player swims out to look.

## The wonders, as the world remembers them

Nobody has the real history. Each is remembered wrong in a way that says something true.

- **Giza** — believed to be flood markers built by people who knew this would happen. They are
  not, but the belief shapes everything: it is why some people think the water will stop.
- **Great Wall** — thought to be a dam that failed. Pilgrims walk its length looking for the
  breach. There is no breach.
- **Colosseum** — assumed to be a giant cistern. Children are told the lions drank there.
- **Machu Picchu** — the model. "Build like that and you live." The upland settlements imitate
  its terraces badly.
- **Chichen Itza** — considered a calendar that predicts the water. People still read it. It
  says nothing.
- **Petra** — carved, not built, so it is believed to be older than the flood and therefore
  proof the world existed before water. A place of argument.
- **Taj Mahal** — universally understood to be a tomb, and universally agreed to be the most
  beautiful thing anyone has ever seen. Nobody argues about the Taj.
